Local Properties of Binary Images
in Two Dimensions
STEPHEN B. GRAY

Abstract-Aspects of topology and geometry are used in analyzing
continuous and discrete binary images in two dimensions. Several
numerical properties of these images are derived which are "locally
countable." These include the metric properties area and perimeter,
and the topological invariant, Euler number. "Differentials" are defined for these properties, and algorithms are given. The Euler differential enables precise examination of connectivity relations on the
square and hexagonal lattices. Easily computable binary image characterizations are introduced, with reference to a serial binary image
processor (BIP) now being built. A precise definition of "localness"
is given, and some implications for image computation theory are
examined.
Index Terms-Binary images, connectivity, Euler number, hexagonal lattice, local properties, neighborhood analysis, perceptron, serial
processors, square lattice, theory of computation, topology.

I. INTRODUCTION

T HIS paper describes certain properties of binary
images in two dimensions, both in the continuous
plane and on the hexagonal and square lattices.'
These numerical properties are locally countable; that is, a
given image property is a sum of numbers, where each
number is derived from data contained in a small piece or
"cell" of the image. (Cells are simply connected, completely
cover the image, are smaller than significant image details,
are all measured by the same process, and are independent
of each other for measurement purposes.)
Of particular interest is the fact that the topological
property "Euler number" of a region of space is locally
countable (Fig. 1). Euler number in two-space is simply the
number of distinct objects, including points, lines, and
blobs, minus the number of holes inside objects. In one dimension, this definition reduces to the number of distinct
segments along a line.
Euler number is useful in image processing both because
the topological counts in a given image are of intrinsic interest in characterizing and recognizing the image, and because Euler number is a key concept in examining connectivity relations of individual bits on lattices. This is discussed
at length below.
In addition to Euler number, certain metric properties
are locally computable, such as perimeter length and area.
Useful image characterizations can be obtained by formulas
involving these measurements.
The concept of summing local measurements is, of course,
closely related to the perceptron idea. Local measurements

A. Bit Quads
We have introduced bit cells, which are squares each
having the value 0 or 1, and cell groups, which are squares
centered on the corners of four bit cells. A bit quad is a
2 x 2 array of bit cells. Clearly the values of the four bit cells
in a quad must be known to count the fundamental quantities no, n1, and n2 in the corresponding cell group.

The local property counter is analogous to a perceptron
each of whose second-level elements computes a weighted
sum of all its first-level outputs, and compares the sum to a

deciding or recognizing, so the threshold step is omitted.
Having defined the LPC, we now define the localness of
a measurement on a binary lattice as the ordered set of
integers {D, G, R, M}, which are the minimum diameter,
degree, order, and dimension on LPC needs to determine
the measurement.

C. Remarks on Localness
The table shows that on an N-dimensional lattice, Euler
number E is a measurement of order N + 1 and diameter 2,
at least for N < 3. (It has been shown that E is of order 4 on
the cubic lattice [4, p. 54].) The quantity N+ 1 is what one
might expect, for N +1 points are required to define N
orthogonal directions. It is interesting to compare this with
a previous result given for two dimensions [1, p. 87]:
E = n[l] - n[11] - n[] + n[1l

(This expression can easily be derived from the E =no
-n1 + n2 argument.) Their expression easily generalizes to
three (and more) dimensions. There would be one "point
term," n [1], three linear terms, three area terms, and a new
volume term, namely - n [2 x 2 x 2 cubes of l's]. Hence, E
in N dimensions is clearly of order R < 2N with D =2. I do
not know of a proof that E is of order exactly N + 1.
All local properties considered in this paper are of diameter 2 or less. Since these properties and the derived characterizations (width, thickness, etc.) are fairly powerful
measurements, and since no useful local properties of higher
diameter are known, it is suggested that diameter-2 neighborhoods are sufficient for powerful numerical image measurements. In Sections IV-C and V-D, we found that diameter-3 neighborhoods are necessary for finding certain cells
in the image (e.g., those having a particular Euler differential, in terms of U'|, or those having a certain neighborhood
